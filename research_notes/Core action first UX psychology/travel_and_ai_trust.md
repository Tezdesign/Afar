# Traveller behaviour, price trust and trust in AI assistants (for a travel deal-finding app with an AI assistant)

Research date: 2026-10-14. Tags: [verified primary] = read in the original document/press release or a verbatim reprint of it; [secondary only] = reported by a news/trade outlet, original not opened; [unverified] = no traceable source; [opinion] = commentary, practitioner or marketing view. ## 1. Verifying "89% want AI help planning but only 12% would let AI decide alone" (Booking.com)

### Takeaway
The two numbers are real, from the same Booking.com survey, but the popular paraphrase distorts both. 89% = "want to use AI in their future travel plans" (not "want AI help planning"); 12% = "feel comfortable with AI making decisions independently" (not "would let AI decide alone"). Sample, year and wording are verifiable.

### Cited Findings
- Source: Booking.com "The Global AI Sentiment Report", press release dated 23 July 2025; online questionnaire, fieldwork April-May 2025, "responses from 37,325 people across 33 markets" (includes UAE, India, USA, UK, EU states, Japan, Brazil etc.; the UAE is the only Middle East/North Africa market in the list, no North African country). [verified primary reprint] — [Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4128268.html)
- Regional split quoted in coverage: Asia-Pacific 93% want to use AI for future travel planning but only 16% comfortable with independent AI decisions. ## 2. Credible surveys on trust in AI trip planning and AI completing bookings (delegation vs suggestions)

### Takeaway
Consistent pattern across vendors and pollsters in 2025-2026: majorities are fine with AI giving suggestions and building itineraries; a small minority (roughly 2-20%, depending on wording) will let AI spend money with no approval; acceptance rises sharply (about 40-45%) when a final-approval step exists. Numbers are not comparable across surveys.

### Cited Findings
- Expedia Group "The AI Trust Gap" (YouGov, "more than 5,700 adults", US/UK/India, fieldwork 10-25 March 2026, post dated 14 April 2026): "68% still prefer to book with a trusted travel brand over AI platforms"; "53% are comfortable letting AI suggest options"; "42% would use AI to monitor prices and determine the best time to book"; "40% would use AI help build itineraries"; "66% ... wouldn't trust AI to buy or book anything on their behalf"; concerns: loss of control 57%, data privacy 57%, misuse of personal data 56%; "Just 8% of travelers rely on AI platforms like ChatGPT and Gemini when planning a trip" vs 59% search engines, 49% OTA. Vendor-run, no question wording or margin published. [verified primary (company blog), vendor-commissioned] — [Expedia Group](https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/resources/blog/ai-trust-gap-why-travelers-continue-to-choose-trusted-brands)

### Inferences
- Design for "AI proposes, user commits": the most defensible stated-preference evidence (HUMAN 43% vs 12%, Amadeus 20% vs 43% conditional) is that a final approval step roughly triples-to-quadruples acceptance of AI-led booking versus none.
- Cross-checking is the norm (Amadeus: ~89% cross-check), so link out to the source fare/hotel and show provenance.
- Stated attitudes overstate behaviour (e.g. 65% interested vs 20% would allow autonomous spend; 16% actually completed a booking via AI).

## 3. Algorithm aversion and appreciation; effect of control/modification

### Takeaway
Aversion appears after seeing an algorithm err; appreciation appears for lay people receiving advice (especially when they have no expertise and aren't choosing between algorithm and own estimate). Letting users modify the output, even slightly, raises uptake (Dietvorst et al. 2018). This supports editable, adjustable AI deal recommendations.

### Cited Findings
- Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015), "Algorithm Aversion: People Erroneously Avoid Algorithms After Seeing Them Err", J. Experimental Psychology: General 144(1). Five studies; people "more quickly lose confidence in algorithmic than human forecasters after seeing them make the same mistake", even when the algorithm outperformed the human. [verified primary abstract via search] — [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/fnce_papers/58)
- Choice rates in the 2015 paper: reviewer reports roughly 60-70% chose the algorithm before seeing it, falling to ~20-40% after seeing it err. [secondary only; approximate] — [Kluver review](https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~kluve018/Algorithm-Aversion-Review.html). Not verified against the paper's tables (attempt to read the Wharton PDF yielded unreadable binary).
- Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2018), "Overcoming Algorithm Aversion: People Will Use Imperfect Algorithms If They Can (Even Slightly) Modify Them", Management Science 64(3):1155-1170, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643. Allowing participants to modify the forecast increased willingness to use the algorithm; Study 3: modification made people more satisfied with the process, more likely to believe the algorithm was superior, and more likely to choose it for later forecasts; effect insensitive to how much they could adjust (10, 5 or 2 units). [verified primary abstract, secondary detail] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v64y2018i3p1155-1170.html); [Wharton summary](https://wpa.wharton.upenn.edu/content/academic-papers/overcoming-algorithm-aversion-people-will-use-imperfect-algorithms-can-even-slightly-modify/)
- Logg, Minson & Moore (2019), "Algorithm appreciation: People prefer algorithmic to human judgment", Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 151:90-103, DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2018.12.005. Six experiments: lay people adhere more to advice they think comes from an algorithm than from a person; appreciation waned when people chose between the algorithm's estimate and their own (Exp. 3) and among experts in the domain (Exp. 4); experts relied less on algorithmic advice, which hurt accuracy. [verified primary abstract via search] — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v151y2019icp90-103.html)
- Proposed reconciliation (one commentary, not a tested finding): Logg et al. studied advice for decisions about others, aversion research studied decisions for oneself. [opinion] — [BU Susilo](https://www.bu.edu/susilo/?p=59106)

### Inferences
- A booking assistant is closer to "choose between algorithm and own judgment, for myself, with money at stake" (the aversion-prone condition) than to the passive-advice paradigm of Logg et al.
- First visible error (wrong visa, wrong price) likely does disproportionate damage; supports making corrections cheap and acknowledging uncertainty.
- Caveat: these are lab forecasting tasks, not travel, and not LLM assistants; transfer is by analogy.

### Gaps
- 2015 percentages and sample sizes not verified from the paper; no travel-specific replication found.

## 4. Human-in-the-loop and confirmation patterns for agents

### Takeaway
Published guidance converges on: preview intended actions, confirm before consequential/irreversible steps, allow correction/interrupt/takeover, show why, and keep a record of what the agent did. Strongest primary source found is Amershi et al. (CHI 2019); vendor and practitioner agent guidance is mostly secondary/opinion here.

### Cited Findings
- Amershi et al. (2019), "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction", CHI 2019 (Microsoft Research, Aether, Office). 18 guidelines in 4 phases: initially, during regular interaction, when wrong, over time; synthesised from 20+ years of work (160+ candidate recommendations per a course summary) and validated in a "rigorous, 4-step process". [verified primary (project page)] — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/)
- Exact wording of individual guidelines (e.g. "support efficient correction", "make clear why the system did what it did") was not retrieved from the paper; I recall them from prior knowledge. [unverified in this session] — check the CHI paper before quoting.
- OpenAI ChatGPT agent (July 2025): asks permission before real-world-consequence actions such as purchases or sending email, offers "takeover mode" so the user enters credentials themselves, refuses high-risk tasks such as bank transfers. Reported through news/guide coverage; OpenAI's own docs not opened. Decrypt notes approval applies "in some cases", not every action. [secondary only] — [AARP](https://www.aarp.org/personal-technology/ready-for-ai-shopping/); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/330714/openais-chatgpt-agent-launches-expanded-powers-elevated-risk); [CX Today](https://www.cxtoday.com/conversational-ai/openais-new-chatgpt-agent-will-find-communicate-with-and-buy-from-businesses/)
- Practitioner patterns for agentic UX: "Intent Preview" and "Autonomy Dial" (plan shown first; proceed / edit / do manually), explainable rationale and confidence signal, action receipts with rollback, pause/stop controls. [opinion] — [Smashing Magazine, Feb 2026](https://smashingmagazine.com/2026/02/designing-agentic-ai-practical-ux-patterns/); [AI UX Playground](https://www.aiuxplayground.com/frameworks/agentic)
- Survey evidence that supports approval steps: 43% vs 12% (HUMAN Security) and 20% unconditional vs 43% conditional (Amadeus); see section 2. [secondary only]

### Inferences
- Minimum viable pattern for an assistant that touches money: preview card (what/price in total/dates/traveller names) -> explicit confirm -> receipt -> link to supplier booking. Start with "find and shortlist", not "book".
- Undo is limited in travel (fare rules, 24h cancellation rules vary by market); so confirmation before the action matters more than undo.

### Gaps
- No Nielsen Norman Group article on agent confirmation/undo was surfaced by search (only practitioner sources); Anthropic and Google first-party agent-UX guidance were not found in this pass. No controlled study on confirmation steps in travel agents found.

## 5. Price transparency: drip pricing, hidden fees, total-price display

### Takeaway
Causal field evidence (StubHub) shows back-loaded fees raise spend and conversion at the cost of trust-relevant harm; regulators in the UK, EU and US have since required total price up front, at least for lodging in the US and UK/EU generally. For a deal-finding app, show all-in price in results and treat "from" prices as a liability.

### Cited Findings
- Blake, Moshary, Sweeney & Tadelis (2021), "Price Salience and Product Choice", Marketing Science 40(4):619-636, DOI 10.1287/mksc.2020.1261. StubHub randomised 50% of US users to back-end fees (shown at checkout) vs upfront all-in pricing. Disclosing fees upfront reduced quantity and quality of purchases; the quality effect accounts for at least 28% of the revenue decline; click-stream suggests shrouding makes comparison harder. Press coverage cited about 21% more spend and ~14% higher purchase completion with back-end fees; those press figures vary by outlet. [verified primary abstract; press figures secondary] — [NBER w25186](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25186.pdf); [Yale Tobin summary](https://tobin.yale.edu/research/price-salience-and-product-choice); [Ticketing Business](https://www.theticketingbusiness.com/2021/02/19/drip-pricing-leads-to-consumers-spending-more-on-tickets-study/)
- FTC Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees: published 90 FR 2066, 10 Jan 2025, effective 12 May 2025; covers live-event tickets and short-term lodging (hotels, motels, inns, short-term and vacation rentals), including third-party platforms, resellers, OTAs and travel agents; requires clear, prominent total price including mandatory fees known and calculable up front; some charges may be disclosed later if before payment; vote 4-1; narrowed from the proposal's all-goods scope. Does not cover flights (airline fees fall under DOT). [verified primary (Federal Register/FTC pages reported via search)] — [FTC final rule PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/r207011_udf_rule_2024_final_0.pdf); [Federal Register](https://govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-10/pdf/2024-30293.pdf). I did not verify later litigation or amendments.
- UK CMA hotel-booking investigation closed 6 Feb 2019 with commitments from Expedia, Booking.com, Agoda, Hotels.com, ebookers, trivago: compulsory charges (taxes, booking/resort fees) must be in headline price; no false scarcity/popularity; no placing sold-out hotels to create urgency; clear ranking-commission disclosure; deadline 1 Sept 2019; CMA made no finding of breach. [verified primary] — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/hotel-booking-sites-to-make-major-changes-after-cma-probe)
- UK DMCC Act 2024: drip-pricing ban in force 6 April 2025: headline price must include fixed mandatory fees; variable mandatory fees disclosed with calculation method; CMA direct fines up to GBP 300,000 or 10% of global turnover per search summary (verify; Act text says 10% of global turnover); final CMA price-transparency guidance November 2025; CMA opened eight investigations and sent advisory letters to 100 businesses in November 2025. [secondary only, law-firm summaries] — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/no-hidden-charges-clamping-down-on-drip-pricing); [SCL](https://www.scl.org/cma-issues-final-price-transparency-guidance-and-launches-first-investigations-under-dmcc-act/); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/dont-forget-about-the-price-tag-price-transparency-under-the-dmcc/)
- EU CPC: Airbnb committed to show total price incl. mandatory fees per listing (2018-19). [secondary only (search summary)] — [European Commission accommodation-booking page](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/consumers/consumer-rights-and-complaints/enforcement-consumer-protection/coordinated-actions/accommodation-booking_de)

### Inferences
- Implication for flight results: show the total including mandatory taxes/carrier charges; label bag/seat add-ons as optional and estimate "cheapest fare + typical bag" separately. Implication for hotels: show per-stay price with taxes and resort fees included, disclose any pay-at-property amounts.
- StubHub shows all-in pricing can reduce short-term conversion; that is the cost of the trust positioning, not evidence trust itself rises. I found no study measuring brand-trust uplift from total-price display in travel.

### Gaps
- Baymard-style usability data on total-price display, and the DOT airline ancillary-fee rule's current status, not retrieved.

## 6. Decision anxiety, "book now or wait", urgency messaging, dark-pattern enforcement

### Takeaway
Regulators have repeatedly acted against false urgency/scarcity in travel (CMA 2019, EU CPC/Booking.com 2019-20, UK DMCC bans on fake countdowns); academic evidence says urgency cues raise booking intention but aggressive use erodes trust. Buy/wait predictors lack independent validation.

### Cited Findings
- CMA 2019 pressure-selling findings and commitments (see section 5). [verified primary] — [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/hotel-booking-sites-to-make-major-changes-after-cma-probe)
- EU CPC dialogue with Booking.com (commitments accepted Dec 2019, implement by 16 June 2020): "last free room" must refer only to Booking.com's own offers; must not present an offer as time-limited when the price remains available afterwards. Booking.com and Expedia Group also committed to change how they present offers, discounts and prices. [secondary only] — [European Commission page](https://commission.europa.eu/topics/consumers/consumer-rights-and-complaints/enforcement-consumer-protection/coordinated-actions/accommodation-booking_de)
- EU CPC sweep Jan 2023 of 399 online shops (retail, not travel): 148 had at least one dark pattern; 42 fake countdown timers; 70 hid important information. [verified primary, press release] — [European Commission IP/23/418](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_23_418/IP_23_418_EN.pdf) (retrieved via search listing; I did not find a travel-specific CPC sweep).
- Academic/conference: Bencsik & Hajdu (2025, CEEeGov, N=247, Booking.com content analysis and survey): time- and demand-based scarcity messages significantly influence booking intentions, especially for younger, impulsive users; aggressive tactics can undermine trust. A 2020 ICIM experiment with 96 participants found limited-time messages produced higher booking intention than limited-quantity ones. Both small conference papers. [secondary only] — [Bencsik & Hajdu PDF](https://real.mtak.hu/231662/1/ceeegov2025-7.pdf); search summary for the ICIM paper.
- Behavioral Scientist piece: participants reacted negatively to scarcity ads, linking it to belief the site had bad intentions, but were resigned. Underlying study not identified. [opinion / unidentified] — [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/consumers-are-becoming-wise-to-your-nudge/)
- Hopper claims "95% accurate" predictions (variously described as within $5 of the fare up to a year ahead, or in backtests with Watch filters); no independent verification found. A 2019 Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management study (Hopper vs KAYAK vs FareHack, 77 days, ten one-way routes) found no single best platform; accuracy varies with days before departure. [Hopper claim: unverified / marketing; academic: verified abstract only] — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1298714); [UNLV record](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/hotel_fac_articles/269)
- Expedia 2026: 42% would use AI to monitor prices and pick the best time to book (demand for buy/wait guidance exists). [verified primary, vendor] — [Expedia Group](https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/resources/blog/ai-trust-gap-why-travelers-continue-to-choose-trusted-brands)

### Inferences
- Any "only 2 left" or "N people viewing" style message in the app needs to be true, current and specific to the exact dates/fare shown to avoid the CMA/CPC fact patterns; safest is to avoid them.
- A buy/wait indicator should show confidence and its basis, and avoid the unqualified "95%" language.

### Gaps
- Could not find a Journal of Travel Research study on scarcity cues and perceived manipulation; no peer-reviewed study quantifying price-volatility anxiety or FOMO in travel booking surfaced; FTC actions on travel dark patterns beyond the fees rule were not searched.

## 7. Visa and passport friction; is passport-aware search a differentiator?

### Takeaway
Entry-rule complexity measurably deters travel (IATA: 36% discouraged), but I found no data on how often travellers are denied boarding or surprised, and no evidence that mainstream metasearch (Skyscanner, Google Flights, Kayak, Booking.com) offers passport-aware visa display. Treat the differentiator claim as plausible but unproven.

### Cited Findings
- IATA Global Passenger Survey (press release 15 Dec 2023; sample size and fieldwork dates not stated on page): 36% of travellers said immigration requirements had discouraged them from visiting a destination; complexity of process the main deterrent for 49%, costs 19%, privacy 8%; where visas required, 66% want to apply online beforehand, 20% prefer consulate/embassy, 14% airport; 87% would share immigration information in advance (83% in 2022). [verified primary] — [IATA](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2023-releases/2023-12-15-01/)
- Timatic (IATA) is the database airlines use to check documents; airlines face fines for carrying passengers without correct documents; ~72 regulation updates per day pre-COVID. [secondary only] — [Wikipedia: Timatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timatic) and IATA Knowledge Hub (not opened)
- Sherpa and Timatic can disagree: a Japan Airlines page warns Sherpa information may not be the most current and that boarding may be refused if documents are incomplete. A British Columbia tribunal (CRT) case turned on a Timatic excerpt allowing transit without a Schengen visa while the airline's tariff rules still applied. Other cases are anecdotal forum posts. [verified primary for JAL page existence; CRT decision secondary] — [JAL](https://www.jal.co.jp/ch/en/info/travelalerts/flysafe/flights-service/quarantine-immigration/); [CRT decision](https://decisions.civilresolutionbc.ca/crt/crtd/en/462376/1/document.do)
- Search found no built-in passport/visa display in Skyscanner, Google Flights, Kayak or Booking.com; third-party tools exist (e.g. EntryCheck Chrome extension overlays visa badges on Google Flights). Booking.com's terms put responsibility on the traveller. Absence of evidence from web search, not a product audit. [unverified / secondary only] — [EntryCheck listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/gfbdmeieifamgheecbmdpadniofodkfa); [Booking.com terms excerpt (ConductAtlas)](https://conductatlas.com/platform/bookingcom/bookingcom-terms-and-conditions/passenger-responsible-for-passport-visa-and-entry-requirements/)

### Inferences
- If competitors really lack passport-aware results, showing "visa-free / eVisa / visa required" per result (per nationality, with a sourced "last checked" date and a disclaimer pointing to the official source) is a credible differentiator, particularly for holders of passports with high visa burden (e.g. many North African nationals). This needs verification by directly testing Skyscanner/Google Flights/Kayak apps.
- The Timatic/Sherpa disagreement evidence argues for a visible freshness date and "verify with the embassy/airline" link, because the app cannot be the legal source of truth.

### Gaps
- No data on the rate of denied boarding or surprised travellers due to visa rules; no quantified evidence that visa display increases conversion or trust; competitor feature audit not done.

## 8. International and MENA context (payments, currency, language/RTL)

### Takeaway
Card and currency friction in North Africa is real and country-specific (Algeria's allowance now loaded on cards; Egypt's card limits; Tunisia's dinar controls). Language matters broadly (CSA Research). I found no travel-specific quantification for MENA and nothing on RTL conversion effects or Morocco.

### Cited Findings
- Algeria (mid-2026): since 19 July the annual travel allowance (EUR 750 adults; EUR 300 minors 12-19, up to two children per family) is credited to a Visa/Mastercard international or dedicated card instead of cash; foreign-currency balances can be used for some online purchases such as hotel and flight bookings; card issuance times are lengthening with demand; opening an international card is not possible for non-workers. [secondary only, Algerian media] — [Dzair Tube](https://www.dzair-tube.dz/en/bank-of-algeria-sets-rules-for-foreign-travel-allowance-via-international-bank-cards/); [Dzair Tube guide](https://www.dzair-tube.dz/en/travel-allowance-cards-in-algeria-a-step-by-step-guide-to-processing-times-fees-and-bank-charges/)
- Egypt (2023-24 reports): banks tightened foreign-currency card use; examples cited include limits around USD 50 cash / USD 100 POS monthly caps at some banks and one bank blocking foreign-currency online purchases with its cards. Dated; current state unverified. [secondary only] — [Techpoint Africa](https://techpoint.africa/2024/01/17/egyptian-banks-impose-card-limits/); [Enterprise](https://enterprise.press/?p=11471)
- Tunisia: La Poste's "e-Dinar Travel" prepaid card (max load 6,000 TND) manages the tourist allowance and can pay hotel booking fees and online services; the International Technological Card (CTI) is aimed at companies/developers; the dinar has convertibility restrictions for individuals; ATM withdrawals for foreign cards are typically 300-600 TND. Sources partly dated (2015, 2019) and disagree on convertibility. [secondary only] — [Idaraty e-Dinar Travel](https://idaraty.tn/fr/procedures/obtention-de-la-carte-internationale-e-dinar-travel-la-poste); [MRI Guide](https://mriguide.com/tunisia-new-currency-limits-for-travelers/)
- CSA Research "Can't Read, Won't Buy B2C" (2020; 8,709 consumers, 29 countries; Kantar panel): 76% prefer to buy products with information in their native language; 40% never buy from websites in other languages. [verified primary press release] — [CSA Research](https://csa-research.com/Blog/cant-read-wont-buy-consumer-language-preferences); [Newswire release](https://www.newswire.com/view/content/survey-of-8-709-consumers-in-29-countries-finds-that-76-prefer-21174283)
- Morocco: no card/currency source found. [no source]

### Inferences
- A deal-finder that only redirects to suppliers (no in-app payment) sidesteps most card-restriction friction but still has to show prices in the user's currency alongside the supplier's charge currency, and warn when a supplier charges in a currency the user's card or allowance may not support.
- Because many North African users need to pay in foreign currency with restricted cards, "price in EUR/USD plus local-currency approximation, with a note that the card is charged by the supplier" is a safer display than a single converted number.
