# Core-action-first holds up, but only on thin evidence

Opening on the core action is a defensible default for Afar, but it rests on a small set of well-supported ideas and a lot of decoration. The ideas that bear directly on the decision are that users start doing before they read, that perceived control matters, and that a login wall before value is costly. The famous "laws" are mostly narrower than their popular versions. Google's homepage is not evidence for any of this: no controlled comparison exists, and its success is confounded by brand, query volume and a one-field task, which makes it a weak analogue for a flight search needing origin, destination, dates and passengers. Leading products mostly hedge, with an input on top and inspiration below, and hardly any publish experiment data. On trust, the strongest evidence says people accept AI that proposes and refuse AI that commits money unasked. The widely quoted Booking.com "89% / 12%" pair is real but is routinely paraphrased wrongly, and the original PDF was not read in this research. Price honesty is backed by the best causal evidence and by regulation. Passport-aware results are plausible as a differentiator but unproven. Nothing found measures Tunisian or MENA traveller trust in AI. Throughout, "evidence" means a cited study or primary document, and "opinion" means my inference or practitioner commentary.

## Only a few psychology principles actually support opening on the task

The evidence splits into three tiers. The first is replicated findings. Fitts's law is strong for pointing tasks ([MacKenzie 1992](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0701_3)). Classic numeric anchoring replicated in Many Labs ([Klein et al. 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2515245918810225)), though incidental anchors did not. The default effect has a meta-analytic mean of **d = 0.68 across 58 studies, with heavy heterogeneity** ([Jachimowicz et al. 2019](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/when-and-why-defaults-influence-decisions-a-meta-analysis-of-defa/)). These govern how to build a first screen (a large reachable field, sensible defaults, "from" prices that anchor), not whether the first screen should be a search field.

The second tier is the evidence that does bear on the decision, and it is moderate at best. Carroll and Rosson's "paradox of the active user" found people start using software at once and skip instructions ([NN/g summary](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/paradox-of-the-active-user/)), and minimalist task-first instruction reportedly beat manuals, though those figures come from secondary summaries of 1980s desktop work ([Instructional Design summary](https://www.instructionaldesign.org/theories/minimalism/)). Perceived control is argued to be a psychological necessity in a narrative review ([Leotti et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2944661)). On tours, NN/g reports that users dismiss instructional overlays quickly and that stacked tips overload working memory, but the article gives no sample sizes ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-instructional-overlay/)). Expertise-reversal research adds that guidance helping novices can hurt experienced users ([Kalyuga 2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3)). On registration, NN/g's qualitative position is that login walls before value are costly ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/login-walls/)), and Baymard's survey of 1,026 US shoppers found **19% (some pages say 18%) abandoned an order because they did not want to create an account** ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/reduce-cart-abandonment)). That is self-reported checkout behaviour, not app first-run. No peer-reviewed experiment on registration walls turned up, and none that compares a task-first home screen with a menu- or landing-first one.

The third tier is the commonly cited material that is overstated or misapplied. Hick's law describes choice reaction time to equiprobable stimuli, and a CHI critique argues the logarithmic fit in interface data cannot simply be read as Hick's law ([Liu et al. 2020](https://researchportal.ip-paris.fr/en/publications/how-relevant-is-hicks-law-for-hci/)). It does not show that fewer options convert better. Choice overload has a mean effect "virtually zero" across 50 experiments ([Scheibehenne et al. 2010](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/37/3/409/1840020)); a rival meta-analysis finds it appears under moderators such as high complexity and unclear preferences ([Chernev et al. 2015 summary](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual)). The Zeigarnik memory advantage failed in a 2025 meta-analysis, though the tendency to resume interrupted tasks survived ([Ghibellini & Meier 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)). Peak-end predicts retrospective ratings but no better than a plain average ([Alaybek et al. 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)), and the first screen is neither peak nor end. Loss aversion is contested between a mean coefficient near 1.96 in risky-choice studies ([Brown et al. 2024](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jel.20221698)) and claims that it is not general ([Gal & Rucker 2018](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3049660)). Jakob's law, progressive disclosure and "time to first value" are practitioner heuristics; I found no quantified support for the last. Several items in this section (Hick 1952, the jam study figures, the nudge meta-analyses, Fitts-on-touch) were drawn from background knowledge, not re-fetched, and should be spot-checked before any is quoted.

My synthesis, which is opinion: the honest case for core-action-first is "the user arrives with a task, most people do not read, and an account adds nothing to a first search," not "Hick's law says fewer choices." Google's own page cannot carry any of this. Even for a one-field engine it is anecdote, and Afar's search needs several fields, so the analogy fails exactly where it matters. The right translation is a form with strong defaults, or a single language line that fills the form.

## Leading products hedge: an input on top, inspiration below

Documented first screens cluster by whether the product's output needs to know the user's taste. Uber's 2016 redesign was framed around one question, "Where to?", with ride types moved after the destination ([Uber newsroom](https://www.uber.com/us/en/newsroom/newriderapp/)). But press coverage reports that a 2019 redesign replaced it with a grid of services (snippet-level source, unverified against Uber) ([It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/news/uber-redesign-digital-270919)), consistent with the view that broader scope forces a chooser. Spotify asks for five or more artists right after first sign-in because the answers improve the home feed ([Spotify Newsroom](https://newsroom.spotify.com/tag/taste-onboarding/)), and Duolingo asks goal and level questions before lessons while letting users start without an account ([Appcues](https://goodux.appcues.com/blog/duolingo-user-onboarding)). These are departures where questions are the input to the core output. For metasearch, the pattern is a search entry with inspiration beneath it: Google Flights pairs the search box with an Explore map ([9to5Google 2019](https://9to5google.com/2019/03/07/google-flights-material-theme/)). A 2023 student critique of Skyscanner's Android home screen found it opened on service choices and promo tiles with no prominent search bar ([IXD@Pratt](https://ixd.prattsi.org/2023/09/design-critique-skyscanner-android-mobile-app/)), a student analysis of unspecified app version, so treat it as a counter-example, not a ruling.

The numbers behind these choices are weak. Duolingo's roughly 20% DAU gain from delaying sign-up is a quote relayed through a blog from an interview, with no design or sample, and the original test predates the interviewee's tenure ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/articles/gina-gotthilf/)). Skyscanner's Smart Lock case study reports price-alert creation 3.8 times higher for users signing in that way, but it is a vendor-authored case study from 2015-16, read only through a search summary, and prone to selection bias ([Google case study](https://developers.google.cn/static/identity/casestudies/skyscanner-smartlock-casestudy.pdf)). Skyscanner's 2026 claim that 60% of early "Explore with AI" users clicked through measures the beta's own users against no baseline ([Travolution](https://www.travolution.com/news/travel-sectors/intermediaries/ai-discovery-among-new-skyscanner-product-releases/)). No A/B result exists for form versus inspiration on any travel product I could reach. Several first-launch behaviours (Booking.com, Hopper, current Kayak, Google Search app, ChatGPT mobile) could not be verified and need a fresh-install check.

AI placement follows the same hedge. Kayak put AI Mode in the top navigation beside Flights, Stays and Cars ([Skift, Oct 2025](https://skift.com/2025/10/15/kayak-ai-mode-natural-language-search/)); Airbnb tested a free-text "What" box inside the search form and called it phase one of a unified assistant ([Skift, Nov 2025](https://skift.com/2025/11/06/airbnb-is-testing-a-what-box-for-natural-language-search/)); Google Flights' deal search lets users describe a trip while standard filters remain ([BGR](https://www.bgr.com/2028290/google-flight-deals-ai-mode-canvas-travel/)). Most hand off to the normal funnel by deep link. Whether any of them shows the AI's interpretation as removable chips is not confirmed in any source I reached, so Afar's chip design cannot claim precedent. The nearest evidence is that NN/g's small 2026 observational study (about nine participants, count inferred) found users wanted control over the search process, went back to search to validate exact prices, and could not tell which source backed which claim ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-search-infoseeking/)), and that Amadeus (vendor, small samples) recommends conversation for ambiguity and structured components once intent is clear ([Amadeus](https://amadeus.com/en/engineering-blog/articles/ai-chatbot-travel-chat-generated-ui-decisional-ux)). Nothing directly tests chips against a chat answer. That a "translate to filters, then show them" pattern fits all of this is my reasoning, not a finding.

## Travellers will let AI propose but not spend, and the 89% / 12% line needs careful wording

The Booking.com figure comes from "The Global AI Sentiment Report", released 23 July 2025, an online questionnaire fielded April to May 2025 with **37,325 respondents across 33 markets**. The reprinted release says "89% want to use AI in their future travel plans" and "Just 12% feel comfortable with AI making decisions independently", with 25% unsure, 10% "very uncomfortable, refusing to trust AI without human approval", and 6% fully trusting AI ([Hospitality Net reprint](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4128268.html)). The popular paraphrases, "89% want AI help planning" and "12% would let AI decide alone", do not appear in that text. Safe wording is: Booking.com's 2025 survey of 37,325 people in 33 markets found 89% want to use AI in future travel plans, but only 12% are comfortable with AI making decisions independently. I could not open the original PDF (it exceeded the fetch limit), so question text, base and margin of error are unverified. Both numbers are stated attitudes, not behaviour. The UAE is the only MENA market listed, and no North African country is. A separate US survey (HUMAN Security, February 2026) also reports 12%, for comfort with AI booking without approval each time, reported secondhand through [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/americans-warm-to-ai-travel-planning-but-wont-hand-over-the-wallet/). The coincidence is probably a source of confusion, and the two should not be merged.

Other surveys point the same way but are vendor-run and not comparable. Expedia and YouGov (more than 5,700 adults in the US, UK and India, March 2026) report 53% comfortable letting AI suggest options and 66% who would not trust AI to book on their behalf ([Expedia Group](https://partner.expediagroup.com/en-us/resources/blog/ai-trust-gap-why-travelers-continue-to-choose-trusted-brands)). HUMAN Security reports 43% comfortable if the AI asks for final approval before payment against 12% without. An Amadeus study reports 65% interested in booking inside an AI assistant but only 20% willing to let it spend within a budget unapproved, with nearly nine in ten cross-checking AI suggestions ([search summary of Amadeus release](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260803712850/en/), original returned 403). A widely repeated "only 2% would let AI book" appears in a Skift article with no source or sample and should not be cited ([Skift](https://www.skift.com/travel-brands-are-building-ai-agents-for-a-consumer-that-doesnt-exist/)). All of these are stated preferences; Skift Research and McKinsey report only 16% of US travellers had actually completed a booking with an AI tool, relayed secondhand.

The experimental literature supplies a mechanism. Dietvorst and colleagues found people lose confidence in an algorithm faster than in a human after seeing it err, and that letting them modify the output, even slightly, raised willingness to use it ([Dietvorst et al. 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v64y2018i3p1155-1170.html)). Logg and colleagues found lay people often prefer algorithmic advice, but that appreciation faded when people chose between the algorithm and their own judgment, and among experts ([Logg et al. 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v151y2019icp90-103.html)). These are forecasting lab tasks, not travel and not language-model assistants, so transfer is by analogy. A booking assistant is closer to the aversion-prone condition (my judgment, for oneself, with money at stake), which makes the first visible error, such as a wrong visa or price, disproportionately costly and editable output valuable. Shipped agents gate purchase and credential entry behind confirmation or handover (Anthropic for Chrome ([Anthropic](https://claude.com/blog/claude-for-chrome)), Google's Gemini Agent ([Gemini Help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/16596215))), citing prompt injection and the cost of error. Undo and activity-log patterns have no shipped-product documentation in what I found, and no controlled study of confirmation steps in travel agents exists. Preview, confirm and log are therefore supported by survey direction and general human-AI guidelines ([Amershi et al. 2019](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/); exact guideline wording not verified), not by a travel experiment.

## Price honesty has the best causal evidence, and it makes labelled sample data a necessity

The strongest causal result in the whole body of notes is from StubHub, where randomising users to back-loaded fees versus all-in prices showed that disclosing fees up front reduced quantity and quality of purchases ([Blake et al. 2021](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25186.pdf)). That means honest pricing costs short-term conversion; no study found measures a brand-trust gain from total-price display in travel. Regulators have moved against back-loaded fees and false urgency: the UK CMA's 2019 hotel commitments required compulsory charges in the headline price and banned false scarcity ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/hotel-booking-sites-to-make-major-changes-after-cma-probe)), the FTC fees rule covers short-term lodging and tickets (not flights) and took effect 12 May 2025 ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/r207011_udf_rule_2024_final_0.pdf)), and the UK's drip-pricing ban has applied since April 2025 (law-firm summaries only: [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/no-hidden-charges-clamping-down-on-drip-pricing)). Urgency cues raise booking intention in small conference studies, with aggressive use risking trust ([Bencsik & Hajdu 2025](https://real.mtak.hu/231662/1/ceeegov2025-7.pdf), N=247). Hopper's "95% accurate" prediction claim has no independent verification, and a 2019 study found no single best price platform ([UNLV record](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/hotel_fac_articles/269), abstract only).

No source directly addresses a prototype that displays sample prices. My reasoning, which is opinion, is that this is the same fact pattern regulators punish (a price or scarcity cue the user cannot rely on), so sample data should carry a persistent visible label and never be paired with urgency language or buy/wait certainty until a real price source is chosen.

## Visa badges and MENA users are promising bets with a missing evidence base

IATA's 2023 survey says **36% of travellers were discouraged from visiting a destination by immigration requirements**, with complexity the main deterrent for 49% ([IATA](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2023-releases/2023-12-15-01/)); sample size and dates were not stated on the page. I found no built-in passport or visa display in Skyscanner, Google Flights, Kayak or Booking.com, but that is absence from a web search, not a product audit, and a third-party extension overlaying visa badges on Google Flights exists ([EntryCheck listing](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/gfbdmeieifamgheecbmdpadniofodkfa)). No data show visa display raises conversion or trust, or how often travellers are denied boarding. Sources of truth also disagree: a Japan Airlines page warns Sherpa information may not be the latest ([JAL](https://www.jal.co.jp/ch/en/info/travelalerts/flysafe/flights-service/quarantine-immigration/)), so any badge needs a freshness date and a link to the official source.

For international users, 76% of 8,709 consumers in 29 countries prefer buying with information in their own language (a 2020 vendor-run survey), and 40% never buy from sites in other languages ([CSA Research](https://csa-research.com/Blog/cant-read-wont-buy-consumer-language-preferences)). Tunisia-specific material is secondary and partly dated: La Poste's e-Dinar Travel prepaid card is capped at 6,000 TND and can pay hotel booking fees ([Idaraty](https://idaraty.tn/fr/procedures/obtention-de-la-carte-internationale-e-dinar-travel-la-poste)), while the dinar has convertibility restrictions for individuals ([MRI Guide](https://mriguide.com/tunisia-new-currency-limits-for-travelers/)). Algeria's allowance rules are summarized only in [Algerian media](https://www.dzair-tube.dz/en/bank-of-algeria-sets-rules-for-foreign-travel-allowance-via-international-bank-cards/). Arabic speech recognition varies by dialect, with one 2025 dataset study reporting among the highest error rates for Algerian Arabic (search summary of [arXiv 2608.21950](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21950)); nothing on Tunisian dialect, noise, Morocco, RTL effects or MENA trust in AI was found. Voice-first Tunisian Arabic is therefore untested territory, not a feature to promise.

## Recommendations tied to the Afar prototype

These are my recommendations; the column on the right names the evidence behind each and how firm it is.

| Prototype element | Recommendation | Basis and strength |
|---|---|---|
| Natural-language search line with editable chips | Keep the line as the first thing on screen, but make the chips the source of truth: every parsed field (origin, destination, dates, travellers) is visible, removable and typeable, and missing required fields show as empty chips to fill, not guessed. Offer a few example prompts under the line. Do not claim this is how Google, Kayak or Airbnb do it. | Control and verification findings (NN/g, small n) and modification raising algorithm use (Dietvorst 2018, lab). Chip precedent unverified; no direct test. Moderate to weak. |
| Core-action-first with no tour | Open on search with no tour and no sign-in. Ask for sign-in at save or alert. Test whether a deals strip below the line helps or distracts. | Task-first and login-wall guidance is moderate to weak (NN/g, Baymard, 1980s studies). Inspiration-versus-form has no experiment, so treat the strip as a hypothesis and instrument it (PostHog is already in the app). |
| Passport-aware results with visa badges | Show badge states (visa-free, eVisa, visa required, unknown) per result with a "last checked" date and an official-source link. Do not state a rule you cannot source; "unknown" is a valid state. Test competitors' apps first before calling it a differentiator. | IATA 36% deterrent (primary); competitor absence and conversion effect unproven; Sherpa/Timatic disagreement argues for the disclaimer. |
| Assistant with Plan / Preview / Confirm cards and activity log | Keep the assistant as a proposer. The Confirm card should list total price, dates, traveller and the supplier link, and the app should never take payment in chat. Log every action the assistant took, in plain language. Place an entry point in the search line itself, not only a floating button. | Approval step raises stated acceptance from 12% to 43% (HUMAN, secondary) and 20% to 43% (Amadeus, secondary); shipped agents gate purchase. Activity-log precedent not found. Placement guidance is commentary, with NN/g noting AI-mode discoverability problems. |
| Sample data labelled until a price source is chosen | Label sample prices persistently on every card and in assistant answers. Add no urgency, scarcity or buy/wait claims. When real prices arrive, show all-in totals with optional extras separate. | StubHub field experiment (strong); CMA, FTC and UK rules (primary or secondary). Labelling itself is my inference. |
| International users, Tunisia and MENA | Support French and Arabic with RTL before any voice feature. Show price in the supplier's currency with a local-currency estimate and a note on who charges the card. Treat voice as optional, and test Tunisian dialect before promising it. Do not quote MENA AI-trust numbers. | CSA language survey (vendor); card and currency detail is secondary and partly dated; no Tunisia data on trust or dialect ASR. Weak. |

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the question more than it settles it. The defensible case for opening on the task rests on how people behave (they act before they read, and want control), not on named laws, and its natural form for Afar is a language line that fills a visible, editable form, since the Google analogy fails for a multi-field search. The trust evidence points in one direction across vendors: propose, preview, confirm, and show your sources. The causal price evidence and the regulatory record point the same way and make labelled sample data a necessity.

Four gaps matter most before treating any of this as proven. No experiment compares task-first with feed-first for travel. Nothing confirms whether competitors show AI interpretation as chips. Visa display and total-price display lack conversion or trust data. And there is no trust, dialect or payment data specific to Tunisian users. Afar's own prototype can generate the missing evidence, by testing chips against a plain answer, the deals strip against none, and visa badges against none, with real Tunisian and MENA participants.
